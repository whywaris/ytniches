Every faceless niche looks saturated when you search it. Type in "history documentary" and you'll see channels with millions of subscribers. Type in "relaxing sleep sounds" and you'll see hundreds of them.

That doesn't tell you much. Big channels existing isn't the problem. The problem is when nobody new can break in.

So stop asking "how many channels are in this niche?" Ask a better question: **are small channels still getting views here?**

## What saturation actually looks like

A niche is saturated for you when three things are true at once:

- The same few channels take almost every view.
- New and small channels post regularly and stay flat.
- Viewers don't click on anything that isn't already a proven format from a big channel.

A niche with 500 channels can still be wide open if small channels keep breaking through. A niche with 20 channels can be closed if those 20 own every search and every recommendation.

You can't see any of this from a search results page. You have to look at what's happening to the small channels.

## Step 1: Find the small channels

Pick a niche you're considering. Find 10 to 20 channels in it that are small. Not tiny, not huge. Channels that are clearly still growing.

Don't only look at search. Open a few videos in the niche and watch what YouTube recommends next to them. That sidebar shows you who YouTube actually pushes, and it often surfaces channels search never shows you.

Write them down. A spreadsheet is fine. You want the channel name, a rough subscriber count, and how often they upload.

## Step 2: Look for outliers on those channels

This is the part most people skip.

For each small channel, look at its recent videos. You're looking for videos that did far better than the channel usually does. We call those outliers. [A full post on outliers is here](/blog/what-is-an-outlier-video).

Say a channel's videos usually get a few thousand views. Then one video gets several times that. That video matters. Something about its topic, its title or its thumbnail pulled in people who weren't already watching the channel.

If you keep finding recent outliers on small channels, that's the best sign a niche still has room. It means YouTube is still willing to push a small channel's video to new viewers when the video is good.

If the small channels all sit flat, video after video, be careful. That's what a closed niche looks like from the inside.

## Step 3: Check the topic depth

A niche can be open and still be a bad pick if you'll run out of ideas.

Try this. Sit down for 30 minutes and write video ideas for the niche. Don't judge them yet. Just write.

If you can't get to 50, the niche is probably too narrow to build a channel on. If you get to 50 easily and keep going, good. You've found a niche with enough depth to publish in for a year.

## Step 4: Be honest about production cost

Faceless doesn't mean effortless. Some niches need hours of research per video. Some need custom animation. Some need footage you'll have to license.

Before you commit, make one video's worth of the work. Write one script. Find the visuals for one video. Time it.

Then ask yourself if you can do that every week for six months. If the answer is no, pick a niche where the answer is yes. A niche you can keep up with beats a "better" niche you quit.

## Step 5: Pick the angle, not just the niche

Most of the room in a crowded niche sits in the angle. "World War 2" is crowded. "World War 2 stories told from the view of one ordinary soldier" is a different channel.

Look at the outliers you found in step 2. What do they have in common? Often it's an angle the big channels ignore. That gap is where a new channel fits.

## A quick checklist

Before you commit to a niche, you should be able to say yes to all of these:

1. I found at least a handful of small channels in this niche.
2. Several of them had recent videos that beat their own average by a lot.
3. I wrote 50 video ideas without forcing it.
4. I made one video's worth of the work and can repeat it weekly.
5. I have an angle the big channels aren't covering.

If you hit all five, stop researching and start making videos. Research feels productive. Publishing is what actually tells you if you were right.
